The Marriott Mistake

A guide to successful prompt engineering.

Patrick Cox 27 June 2024 2 min read

Imagine you are visiting Philadelphia for a conference and staying at the Marriott. After a long flight you hop in the rental and begin the short trip to the hotel. But thanks to canyon-like roads densely packed with tall buildings, your GPS fails. After your third loop past Liberty Hall, you realise you need a new plan. Frustrated, you ask a police officer, and he guides you succinctly and directly to the parking garage adjacent to the Marriott. After gathering your things you head to the front desk to check in — only to find you don't have a reservation at that Marriott. There are three in Philadelphia.

The lesson: it is really important to ask the right question.

Writing prompts for large language models is very similar. Unless you ask for directions to the exact Marriott where you have a reservation, your odds of success are only 33%. The usefulness and accuracy of results improve with the clarity and specificity of the prompt.

Structure every prompt

Because of the way models like ChatGPT were trained, there are specific prompt structures that consistently produce the intended output. Each prompt should:

  1. Assign the model a role, from which its perspective, approach and tone will be derived.
    • "You're a cardiothoracic surgeon preparing for a lecture on ventricular stents for an upcoming conference."
    • "You're a fifth-grade teacher preparing a lesson plan on the states of matter."
  2. Assign a task — what exactly would you like the model to do?
    • "List the top five ventricular stents and their specifications."
    • "Draft a lesson plan that introduces solids, liquids and gases."
  3. Specify the desired format.
    • "Display in a table with product names as columns."
    • "Create a PowerPoint outline with three embedded videos."
  4. Set parameters — time periods, geographies, distribution channels.
    • "Focus only on stents approved by the FDA since 2020."

Best practices

  1. Provide the model with an example when a specific output format is desired.
  2. Use ### or ''' to separate the task from examples or output parameters.
  3. Be directive, not restrictive. Focus on what you want, not what you don't want.
  4. Work backward from your goal — reverse-engineer your prompt.
  5. Segment long, cumbersome requests into parsed chunks.
  6. Ask for citations, for better reliability and context.
  7. Define success — provide goals.
    • "Reduce bounce rate on the homepage by improving engagement."
  8. Use realistic scenarios.
    • "You're a marketing consultant pitching ChatGPT to a CPG brand to analyse trade promotion ROI."
  9. Encourage analysis.
    • "Compare ChatGPT and Perplexity's capabilities in evaluating marketing attribution models and provide a recommendation."

Large language models are incredibly powerful research tools when used effectively. Asking better questions yields better answers. Best of luck in your discovery — and happy prompting.
